Start in November, while this season's tapes are still fresh. The concept is settled by January, the music is chosen by early March, all wind music is in your hands by May 1, and drill is finished by Labor Day. Each of those dates exists because something else cannot start until it is done.

If you are reading this in spring, skip to the section on starting late.

![The seven stages of designing a show, laid against the school year from November to Labor Day](../how-to-design-a-marching-band-show/fig-calendar.svg "The design year. Two dates hold it up: music by May 1 and drill by Labor Day.")

## Why the calendar runs backwards from two dates

**Labor Day: the drill is done.** The band learned the opener at camp and has been adding a movement every two or three weeks since. Drill that arrives after Labor Day gets taught in the same weeks you need for cleaning, and contests have already started.

**May 1: the wind music is done.** Three things wait on it. Your students need it before spring camp so they can read it down before summer. Your percussion writer needs the finished wind score to write to. And your drill writer cannot count a single set until the music is final, because every set is tied to a count.

Everything earlier in the year is set by working back from May 1.

## The year, month by month

### November: recap and decide who is designing

Sit down with your staff the week after your last contest and go through the season caption by caption. What worked. What you would change. Which sections carried the show and which were covered up. Write it down; you will not remember it in January.

Then decide who is designing next year. December and January are an arranger's quiet months, so a show agreed before winter break starts being written within about two weeks. By February every designer's spring is filling.

If your previous designer's music was late or the pieces of the design never fit together, this is the month to say so and to ask the next one direct questions: when is each part due, who writes percussion, and do your writers talk to each other.

### December: get a number to your boosters

Most boosters set their budget in January or February, and they need a figure before they can vote. Ask for an itemised quote in December: winds, percussion, sound design, drill and coordination each on its own line, with licensing listed separately because the school usually pays it. Ask for a draft contract you can carry to the meeting.

Ask about payment timing too. If your design money comes from stipends that are released in late November, or from a fundraiser in March, a designer who knows that can schedule the deposit around it.

### December and January: concept and cut tracks

One or two calls. Bring your strengths-and-weaknesses chart, the staff's playlist and any idea you already have. A designer should bring two or three concepts and draft material for you to react to; one director told us no designer had done that legwork for him before.

The output is a cut track: the source recordings spliced to real show length, in three or four versions. Agree on the pacing there, where changing your mind is cheap.

### February: eighth-grade night and the first numbers

You will not know your roster until summer, but February is when you learn the most about it. Eighth-grade night tells you roughly how many are coming. An announced show helps: in districts where students have to choose between activities, the show itself is the recruiter.

Start the licensing requests the week the music is chosen. Bands of America's handbook says to submit requests at least three months before you plan to start arranging ([2026 handbook, section 2.03](https://marching.musicforall.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2026/05/2026-BOA.Rulebook-FINAL.pdf)). Many songs clear in a day through a licensing agent. Some take weeks, and some are refused, so have an alternative for anything recent or famous.

### By early March: the music is chosen

Every piece is named, the cut track is approved, and the arranger has your instrumentation. If percussion needs more lead time, aim for March 1.

### March and April: the book is written

Winds come first, a movement at a time, and you should hear drafts. Send each approved wind movement straight to the percussion writer. April and May, after indoor season ends, is when percussion writers have time, and a percussion book that starts late delays everything after it.

Sound design is written alongside, not afterwards.

### May 1: wind music delivered

Read it down with the band at spring camp. Then send your edits as one written list, not as texts across three weeks. Small edits through the summer are normal. Changing a piece after the drill is written means rewriting the drill for that part.

June 1 is the outside edge, for a part or two, and only with a reason.

### June: the roster settles

Rookie camp and summer registration tell you who you have. Lock your equipment and props now too: a drill writer needs to know what is on the field before writing around it.

### July 1: numbers to the drill writer

Drill follows the roster. Send exact numbers by section, your field (hash marks, where the pit sits, anything odd about the stadium), your props with dimensions, and your camp dates with how many sets a day your band can learn.

### Mid-July to Labor Day: drill, a movement at a time

Movement one arrives about July 15, in time for camp. The rest follows every two to three weeks. Re-check your numbers with the drill writer before each movement; a band that lost four clarinets in August should not be learning drill written for the June roster. Choreography arrives with the drill, as video the students can learn from.

### September and October: clean, compete, adjust

Mid-season edits are part of the job. A passage that is not there two weeks before championships gets its easier version. A transition that reads badly from the box gets restaged.

## What happens when a date slips

These are real seasons directors described to us.

| What slipped | What it cost |
|---|---|
| Drill arrived at 4 a.m. on the day it was to be taught | A rehearsal spent reading charts instead of teaching them |
| Percussion came six weeks after winds | Every later date moved; the band started the season behind |
| Drill arrived late and in pieces | The guard instructor could not write choreography without it |
| Sound files arrived the week of the home show | The mix was never balanced that season |
| Concept was not settled until spring | Music was locked before the visual designer had a say |
| Roster was not re-checked before the last movement | Holes in the closer's forms |

A late date rarely costs you only that date. It costs whoever was waiting on it.

## If you are starting late

**It is January or February.** You are on time for custom if you sign now. Do not spend a month deciding.

**It is March or April.** Custom music by May 1 is not realistic, and a designer who promises it is writing your show fast. Buy a finished show and have it re-scored for your band, or take two finished parts and have the rest written around them.

**It is May or later.** Buy a finished show as written and put your effort into drill, which still has to be written for your numbers. Tell the drill writer your camp dates on the first call.

**You have a new director, or you are one.** You may have inherited music that is already locked. Meet the designers once, as a group, in your first two weeks, and ask what is still open.

## A checklist to keep everyone honest

- November: season recap written down; designer chosen
- December: itemised quote and draft contract to the boosters
- January: concept agreed; cut track approved
- February: licensing requests submitted; eighth-grade night
- Early March: all music chosen
- May 1: wind music delivered and read down
- June: equipment and props locked
- July 1: final numbers to the drill writer
- July 15: movement one drill
- Labor Day: all drill delivered

---faq---
Q: Is November too early to plan next year's show?
A: No. It is the best month to recap this season and choose a designer, because arrangers start writing in December and January.

Q: When should marching band music be finished?
A: By May 1, so the band can read it before summer and the drill writer can start. June 1 is the latest workable date.

Q: When should drill be finished?
A: By Labor Day, delivered a movement at a time from mid-July so the band learns the opener at camp.

Q: How long does music licensing take?
A: Many songs clear within a day through a licensing agent, and some take weeks or are refused. Bands of America advises submitting at least three months before arranging starts.

Q: What if we do not know our numbers yet?
A: Design the music to ability, which you know in January, and write drill to the roster, which you know in July. Re-check numbers before each drill movement.

Q: Can we still get a show in April?
A: Yes, a finished one. Custom music started in April will not be ready by May 1.
