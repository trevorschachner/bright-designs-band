Buy a box show if your band is still building its teaching, if it is past February, or if you have found a show that already fits. Commission a custom show when the band can teach what gets written for it and you can start in December. Most bands that ask us this question end up with the third option: a finished show re-scored for their players, with one part swapped or written new.

Directors call them box shows, stock shows or package shows. Sellers call them pre-written. They are the same thing: a complete show that was written for another band and is now for sale.

## What a box show is good at

**It exists.** You can hear all of it today. One director told us he had spent weeks listening to package shows because buying is easy: click, and you have it. That certainty is worth a lot in a job with very little of it.

**It has been played.** A show that went on a field has had its transitions tested, its ranges sung through by real students, and its problems found by someone else's band.

**It costs less,** because the writing is already done.

**It arrives now.** Music in January means winter sectionals, a spring read-through and a drill writer who can start on time.

We know one band that ran box shows on purpose for several years. The director wanted to fix the teaching and the rehearsal process first, with music he did not have to manage. Only then did he ask whether design was the next step. That is the right order.

## What goes wrong with a box show

Directors are not hostile to box shows. Their complaints are specific.

**Fit.** The show was scored for a band with eight trumpets and a strong mellophone line, and you have three trumpets and saxes. Directors describe it as "not a great fit, not bad."

**Sameness.** A popular box show gets marched by several bands in a season. Ask the seller whether you will be the only band in your circuit performing it. Ours are sold to one band per circuit per season.

**The visual comes after.** Drill written for a box show is written to finished music by someone who was not in the room when the music was chosen. Many box shows are sold without drill at all. Ours are: the music, percussion and sound design are finished, and your drill is written for your numbers and your field.

**Nobody adjusts it.** The files arrive and the relationship ends. When part two is not working in September, there is no one to call.

Two of those four are fixable at the point of sale, which is what the hybrid is for.

## What a custom show is good at

**It is written for the band you have.** The book starts from a chart of your sections: who is strong, who is thin, who your soloists are. Your saxes cover your mellophones because the arranger knew they had to.

**The demand is placed on purpose.** One or two hard moments your best players can win, and everything else written to go on the field fast.

**The staff designs it together.** The guard designer hears the cut track before a note is written. The drill writer can ask for sixteen more counts and get them.

**It is yours.** Directors say they want a show that is "uniquely us" and a book the kids deserve. Students know when a show was made for them, and that is where buy in starts.

**It teaches what you want taught.** If this is the year the brass learns to double tongue, the show can be built around that.

## What goes wrong with a custom show

Price is not the main complaint. Across our calls with directors coming from another designer, the complaints were about time and about people.

**Lateness.** "Where's my music." One band received drill at 4 a.m. on the day it was due to be taught. Another got its wind book early and then waited six weeks for percussion, which held up everything after it. Sound files for one large band arrived the week of its home show.

**Designers who never talk to each other.** Directors call it silos. Music gets locked before the visual designer has a say, and the guard ends up placed wherever is left.

**Written too hard.** A show can flatter the designer and bury the band. At one large school, incoming woodwinds looked at the opener, decided they could never play it, and stopped practising.

**It asks more of you.** A custom show needs your feedback on drafts, your numbers on time and a staff that can teach new material. The directors who were unhappy in November were, almost every time, the ones who never commented on a draft.

None of these is a reason to avoid custom. They are the questions to ask a designer before you sign: when is the music due, who is writing percussion, do your writers meet, and what happens if a date slips.

## The hybrid most bands should look at first

There are three points between a box and a blank page.

![A line from box show to full custom with three stops between: as written, re-scored with one piece swapped, and two existing parts plus new ones, showing what changes at each stop](fig-box-to-custom.svg "Five ways to buy a show. Each step to the right adds fit and adds time.")

**As written.** The whole show, played as delivered.

**Re-scored, with a swap.** The show is re-scored to your instrumentation and one piece is replaced with something your students love. This is the option directors liked most when we described it, along with getting edits back within two weeks and video feedback during the season.

**Partial custom.** Keep two or three finished parts and have the rest written new. Aromatic, which we wrote for Gilbert High School in 2025, was built this way: it uses the opener from Spices, Perfumes & Toxins (Alpharetta High School, 2023) with a new ballad and closer. Three existing parts and one new one still make a show no other band has.

You can also build from single arrangements: an opener from one show and a ballad from another, rebalanced so they sit together.

One legal point applies to every option. An arrangement of a copyrighted song is licensed to a specific ensemble. Buying or borrowing another band's chart does not carry the license with it; your band needs its own permission for each copyrighted piece ([Music for All's copyright guidance](https://marching.musicforall.org/adjudication/)). A reputable seller handles that request with you before the music ships.

## Seven questions that decide it

1. **What month is it?** Before January, everything is open. After March, custom music by May 1 is not realistic and you should buy something that exists.
2. **Can your staff teach a show nobody has taught before?** If last year's show took every rehearsal you had, start with a show that is finished and proven, and spend the difference on clinicians and instruments.
3. **Is there a show that already fits?** If you can hear your band in an existing show, buy it. Custom is for when you cannot.
4. **Do you have a concept you cannot find anywhere?** That is the strongest reason to commission.
5. **What does your instrumentation look like?** The further you are from a standard band (no battery, six brass, a huge guard), the more a re-score or a custom book is worth.
6. **How stable are your numbers?** If you could lose or gain twenty students by August, you need a designer who will adjust, whichever kind of show you buy.
7. **What do your boosters need to see, and by when?** A box show has a fixed price you can put in front of a treasurer today. A custom quote takes a call and usually a second one.

## What does not change

Whichever you choose, you still need drill written for your numbers, a license for every copyrighted piece, a sound system that works, and a season of rehearsal. A box show does not save you from any of those, and a custom show does not do them for you.

And the calendar is the same. Music should be in the students' hands before spring camp, and drill finished by Labor Day. A box show bought in June is later than a custom show started in December.

---faq---
Q: What is a box show?
A: A complete marching band show that was written for another band and is now for sale. Directors also call it a stock show, a package show or a pre-written show.

Q: Is a custom show always better than a box show?
A: No. A box show that fits your band, taught well, beats a custom show that arrives late or is too hard. Custom is better when you need music written around your players and you have the time and staff to use it.

Q: Can a box show be changed for my band?
A: Often, yes. Ask whether the seller will re-score it for your instrumentation, swap a piece, or write a new part. Any new arrangement of a copyrighted song needs its own license.

Q: Will another band in my area march the same box show?
A: Ask before you buy. Some sellers limit a show to one band per circuit per season, and some do not.

Q: Does a box show include drill?
A: Sometimes. Many are music only, because drill has to be written for your numbers and your field.

Q: How late can I buy a show?
A: A finished show can be bought into the spring. For custom, start in December or January so the music can be delivered by May 1.
