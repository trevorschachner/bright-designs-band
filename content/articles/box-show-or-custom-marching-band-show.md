Some of the bands that call us about a custom show should buy a box show. We tell them that. It can be one of ours or somebody else's, I don't mind which, but a few times a year the right answer to "can you write us a show?" is "not yet."

I write custom shows, so take that for what it's worth. Here's how Brighton and I think about it.

## What I mean by a box show

A box show is a finished show that was written for another band and is now for sale. You'll also hear stock show or package show. Sellers say "pre-written," which is a word I have never once heard a director use on a call.

Every show in our catalog started as a custom show for one specific band. After their season it goes on the shelf.

## Why box shows are good, and I mean that

One director told us he'd spent the past couple of weeks listening to package shows, and what he kept coming back to was how easy it is. You click buy and you've got it. It's there.

He's right. You can hear the whole thing today. Somebody else's band already found the bad transition in part two. And you get to skip what Ryan calls "all that creative dialogue," which is a real cost if you're a one-person staff with a concert band to run.

We know a director who marched box shows on purpose for several years. He wanted to fix how his band rehearsed before he spent money on design. Then he called us. That's the right order, and I wish more people did it that way.

## Where they let you down

A friend of mine coordinates a lot of bands. For years he sent his small schools to the same popular catalog, because it's good and it's proven. Last fall he told me he'd stopped. The catalog hadn't gotten worse. It had gotten popular, and he could hear it in band after band at his state contest.

That's the first problem: a good box show gets marched a lot. Ask the seller who else in your circuit has it. We sell each of ours to one band per circuit per season, and you should expect that from anyone.

The second problem is fit. The show was scored for a band with eight trumpets and a mellophone line, and you've got three trumpets and a lot of saxes. Directors describe the result to us as "not a great fit, not bad," which is about the saddest review a show can get.

The third is that nobody's home. The files show up and that's the end of the relationship. When part two isn't working in September, there's no one to text.

## What custom gets you

A book written for the kids you have. We start with a chart of your sections: who's strong, who's thin, who your soloists are. If your saxes need to cover your mellophones, they do, because we knew that in January.

You also get a say. Ryan, who coordinates for us, describes how we work as strong opinions, loosely held. We'll show up with reasons for every choice. You should never feel stuck with a tune you don't love.

And the students can tell. Directors tell us they want a show that's "uniquely us," and a book the kids deserve.

## Where custom lets you down

I'd love to say it's the price. It isn't. When a director comes to us from another designer, the story is nearly always about time.

One band got drill at 4 a.m. on the day they were supposed to teach it. One director told us he'd find himself in August asking where part three was, and then he'd have to take whatever first draft showed up, because there was no time left to do anything else.

The other story is designers who've never met. The music gets locked, then a drill writer reacts to it, then the guard gets put wherever is left.

So if you go custom, ask two questions before you sign. When is every piece due? And do your writers talk to each other? On our end, the music is in your hands by May 1, and if somebody on our team is late, that's on us and we make it right.

Custom also asks more of you. You have to answer when we send a draft. The directors who were unhappy in November were almost always the ones who never told us what they thought in March.

## The middle, which is where most bands should look

There's a lot of room between a box and a blank page.

![A line from box show to full custom with three stops between: as written, re-scored with one piece swapped, and two existing parts plus new ones, showing what changes at each stop](fig-box-to-custom.svg "Five ways to buy a show. Every step to the right adds fit and adds time.")

When a band tells us they're leaning box show, here's what we usually offer. We pull a show off our shelf, re-score it for their instrumentation, and swap out a piece so it isn't completely recycled. They get something that fits and that no one else has, without starting from zero. On price it lands in between: a finished show from us is around $2,500, and custom starts at $5,000. The [cost breakdown](/blog/how-much-does-a-custom-marching-band-show-cost) has the rest.

Aromatic is one of these. We wrote it for Gilbert High School in 2025. The opener came from Spices, Perfumes & Toxins, which we'd written for Alpharetta two years earlier, and the ballad and closer were new.

One thing that's true for every option on that line: an arrangement of a copyrighted song is licensed to one band. Buying another band's chart doesn't bring the license with it. Your school needs its own permission for each tune, and a seller worth buying from will sort that out with you before the music ships.

## How I'd decide

**What month is it?** Before January, everything's open. After March, we can't get you custom music by May 1 and I won't pretend we can. Buy something that exists. The whole calendar is in [when to start planning](/blog/when-to-start-planning-a-marching-band-show).

**Did last year's show take every rehearsal you had?** Then buy a finished show, get it in January, and spend the difference on clinicians and instruments. You'll be a better band next fall, and then call us.

**Can you hear your band in a show that already exists?** Buy it. That's what it's there for.

**Do you have an idea you can't find anywhere?** That's the best reason to go custom.

**Is your band an odd shape?** No drumline, six brass, a guard bigger than the wind section. The further you are from a standard band, the less a box will fit. We wrote up what works for [small bands](/blog/marching-band-shows-for-small-bands) separately.

Either way you still need drill written for your numbers, a license for every copyrighted tune, and music in the kids' hands before spring camp. A box show doesn't get you out of any of that. And one bought in June is later than a custom show started in December.

---faq---
Q: What is a box show?
A: A complete marching band show that was written for another band and is now for sale. Directors also call it a stock show or a package show; sellers call it pre-written.

Q: Is a custom show always better?
A: No. A box show that fits, taught well, beats a custom show that's late or too hard. Custom is worth it when you need music written around your players and you have the time and staff to use it.

Q: Can a box show be changed for my band?
A: Often. Ask whether the seller will re-score it for your instrumentation or swap a piece. Any new arrangement of a copyrighted song needs its own license.

Q: Will another band near me march the same show?
A: Ask before you buy. We sell each show to one band per circuit per season. Not everyone does.

Q: What does a box show cost next to a custom show?
A: A finished show from us is around $2,500 for winds, percussion and sound design. Our custom shows start at $5,000, and most high-tier arrangers charge $8,000 to $15,000.

Q: Does a box show come with drill?
A: Usually not. Drill has to be written for your numbers and your field.

Q: How late can I buy one?
A: A finished show can be bought into the spring. For custom, talk to us in December or January.
