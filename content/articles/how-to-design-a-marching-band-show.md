A marching band show is designed in this order: the band you will have, the world of the show, a cut track, the wind book, percussion and sound together, drill, then guard and choreography. Most shows that fall apart in October were designed out of that order, usually with the music finished before anyone asked what the field would look like.

This is the process we use on every custom show, with the rules of thumb we repeat on design calls. It is long because the job is long. Read the part you are in.

![The seven stages of designing a show, laid against the school year from November to Labor Day](fig-calendar.svg "The design year. Music is due May 1 and drill by Labor Day; every other date is set by working back from those two.")

## 1. Start with the band you will have in October

Every design decision depends on who is standing on the field, so the first document is a strengths-and-weaknesses chart, not a playlist.

List each section. Next to it write how many you expect, how strong they are, and who the standout players are by name. In January, ability is more reliable than headcount: you know your rising junior clarinets can play, and you do not yet know how many freshmen will show up to rookie camp. Design to ability first and fix the numbers later.

Then add three things most charts leave out.

**Your one to five best players.** They will be featured as often as the show allows. A strong soloist at a microphone carries a phrase so the rest of the band does not have to, and large bands use the same move for the same reason.

**What you want the band to learn this year.** Double tonguing, a scale, a range. A show can be written to teach those, and it is easier to rehearse a skill that lives in the show than one that lives in a warm-up packet.

**Where the program should be in three to five years.** Ask which season will be your golden year, the one where 40 to 50 percent of the band are juniors and seniors. Design this year's show as a step toward that one. A band that tries to play its golden-year show two years early spends the fall surviving it.

## 2. Build a world, not a plot

A known story cannot be told in eight minutes on a football field. Design a place the students want to live in for a season: what it looks like, what it sounds like, how it changes from the first note to the last picture.

Second Nature, which we wrote for Yorktown High School in 2026, is an example. The idea is one sentence: our phones have become the way we see the world. There is no character and no plot. There is a world in balance (Jónsi's Clearing), a takeover (Mason Bates' Mothership), a stark ballad (Vienna Teng's The Hymn of Acxiom), and a return. Each part changes what the field looks like, and the audience can follow it with the sound off.

The tests we run on a concept:

- **Can you say it in one sentence?** If it needs a paragraph, it is not ready.
- **Will these students relate to it?** A concept that the staff love and the kids do not understand gets no buy in, and buy in is what makes a show clean.
- **Does your personnel rule it in or out?** A witch show wants a sabre line and a soloist who can sell it. A show built on a single lead character is a risk for any band whose lead might quit in September.
- **Can you say how it starts and how it ends?** A show with a strong middle and no ending is an idea in doubt.
- **If the title puns on a famous song, are you playing that song?** The audience will wait for it all night.

Two things directors worry about that matter less than they think. A common concept is fine: a judge we learned from told us the show that gave him goosebumps one Saturday was the third ocean show he had seen that morning. And fun shows are not a step down. A fun show with music the crowd knows builds buy in and recruits eighth graders, and a band can grow into mature material once the teaching is in place.

If you have no concept at all, start from the other end. Find two or three pieces the whole staff is passionate about. A concept can usually be built to fit them.

## 3. Make a cut track before anyone writes a note

A cut track is the source recordings spliced together at real show length, with time left for transitions. It is the cheapest tool in show design and the one most often skipped.

We build three or four versions. Each one answers the questions that are expensive to answer later: how long is the opener, where is the first big hit, how long does the ballad sit, how does the show end. Changing your mind on a cut track costs an afternoon. Changing it after the wind book is written costs weeks, and after the drill is written it costs the drill.

The cut track is also where the staff gets on the same page. The guard designer hears where the flag feature is. The percussion writer hears how long the front ensemble carries a transition. Nobody is reacting to a finished score in May.

Listen for the shape. Across the 33 four-part shows in our catalog, the parts settle into a consistent pattern.

![Median length of each part across 33 four-part shows: part one 1:32, part two 2:08, ballad 2:05, closer 1:57](fig-show-shape.svg "Median part lengths across the 33 four-part shows in our catalog. Whole shows run from 5:00 to 8:43, and half of them fall between 7:00 and 8:00.")

The first part is short. Its job is to get to the first impact and give the audience a reason to clap. If the first applause comes at 1:45, it is late; aim to have the first impact and the transition out of it done by about 1:30. Part two is the longest and carries the most content. The ballad earns two minutes. The closer is shorter than directors expect, because a closer needs a couple of strong moments and an ending, not a third opener.

Shorter is safer. Plan 6:30 to 7:30 and leave leeway. Thirty extra seconds gains nothing on a score sheet, and five fewer drill sets fully taught beat five more sets half taught.

## 4. Write one seven-minute piece, not four movements

The shows that lose points lose them between the movements. The music stops, the band shuffles to a new set, a metronome clicks from the pit, and for twelve seconds nothing is being performed.

**Sound never drops.** A drone, a front ensemble texture or a sample carries through every key change and after the ballad's last chord. The audience should not be able to point to the place where part two ended.

**Every transition answers three questions.** Is there enough time to move? What is the front ensemble doing? What sound carries us to the next idea? If one of the three has no answer, the transition is not written yet.

**Write transitions long.** Four to eight bars. Cutting two bars in August is easy. Cramming a restage into a transition that was written too short is not.

**Plan an effect every 15 to 20 seconds.** Put the show on a timeline, rehearsal letter by rehearsal letter, and mark what the audience gets at each point: a hit, a solo entrance, a colour change, a prop reveal, a held picture. Gaps on that timeline are where attention wanders.

![A show timeline for Second Nature with its four parts, a marker for the first impact before 1:30, and a tick every 20 seconds where an effect is planned](fig-show-map.svg "A planning grid laid over the real part lengths of Second Nature (8:10). The ticks are where to ask the question; what goes on each one is the design.")

Some structure rules that save a rewrite:

- Do not open cold on exposed rhythmic material. Give the band a sustained or simple entrance to settle on.
- A percussion feature belongs in the 30 to 45 seconds after the first impact, or straight after the ballad where it can cover a restage. Pair it with a soloist.
- After a serious ballad, change pace. Something lighter, something percussion driven, or a full band and guard unison.
- A closer built from three source pieces reads as disjointed. Use one or two.
- If your show has four ideas, let the opener melt into part two so the audience hears three movements.
- Vary the soloist-then-band pattern. Small bands get marked down for repeating it, even though larger bands are praised for the same thing.

## 5. Spend your rehearsal time on purpose

We call it the 70/30 rule. Build one or two moments that are hard and worth it, and plan to spend about 70 percent of the season's rehearsal on them. Everything else has to go on the field almost the first time.

![Two bars comparing how a season's rehearsal is spent: on purpose, 70 percent on two hard moments and 30 on the rest; by accident, spread evenly across a show that is hard everywhere](fig-seventy-thirty.svg "The 70/30 rule. A show that is hard everywhere leaves no time for fundamentals, and the weak fundamentals show up in every caption.")

Directors hear this as permission, and it is. Most of the show can be grade 2 or 3 ensemble writing that your second trumpets can play in the cold. The demand lives in a few places you chose.

**You cannot write a band into sounding better, but you can write them out of it.** Scoring up to flatter a band has the opposite effect. Simpler writing makes the same students sound better.

**Put the hard passage early in the show.** The opener gets taught at camp and rehearsed every week after. A hard passage in the closer gets half the weeks.

**Give the hardest material to soloists and small ensembles.** They practise alone, it works, and it costs you no full-ensemble rehearsal.

**Write demand you can retreat from.** If there is a double-tongued run, write the eighth-note version at the same time. If the run is not there two weeks before championships, the fallback is already in the folder and nobody has to learn a new part.

**Aim for difficulty and ability to meet at the end of the season.** The right hard passage is one the band reaches in late October, not one they can play at camp and not one they never reach.

**Use mixed metre in doses.** About 25 seconds, then back to something regimented. Give it to the percussion as a transition, or let the winds play it standing still. Do not ask a band to march in seven.

A show that is too hard eats the time you would have spent on basics. One director told us that once school started, rehearsal was almost all show and no fundamentals, and the top tenth of the band carried it. A test before delivery: estimate each movement's share of rehearsal, and ask whether this staff can teach this drill page in an hour.

## 6. Score for the band you have

The book is written from the chart in step one, section by section.

- **Never make the brass and woodwinds compete.** Split highs from lows and woodwinds from brass so each has room to be heard.
- **Score everyone in their power range.** A part that sits where the instrument sounds good is louder and more in tune than a part that sits high.
- **Save a small brass section for impacts.** Loudness is an effect. If the trumpets play all the time, the hit is not a hit.
- **Small bands need thicker scoring at the hits.** A unison stab lands with 150 players. With 40 it needs more notes in the chord.
- **Move sustained chords into the front ensemble or the electronics.** The soloist carries the line and the winds get to breathe.
- **Cover weak sections instead of exposing them.** Saxes can double weak mellophones. A new bass drum line can get a pulse-only book. A thin high brass section gets little that is exposed.
- **Write tempos the band will reach.** A closer played under tempo runs long and makes every sustained note harder. Pop tunes usually want lifting to about 144 to 152. A movement near 120 is a welcome break in a show that otherwise lives between 152 and 172.

For woodwinds in particular: give them the lyrical melody early in the ballad, keep clarinets from crossing the break in fast passages, and notate a flute forte-piano as a short note, a rest, then the crescendo, because that is how it will be played anyway.

For a developing band, make it easy on purpose. About three tempos in the whole show, keys of F, B-flat and E-flat, one brass moment and two woodwind moments, and no battery in the ballad.

## 7. Start from the picture

Ask the staff what they want to see, with no music in mind, and then find out what it sounds like. A good show reads with the sound off. If the visual is added to finished music, the guard ends up wherever is left.

This is why we prefer to have the music and the drill written by one team. When the drill writer needs sixteen more counts to get the band across the field, the arranger adds them. When those two people have never met, the band marches a step size that hurts the playing.

**Stage soloists as a whole moment.** Know who each soloist is a minute before they play. Pull them out of the form about 30 seconds early and restage the band behind them. Give every soloist one fixed spot with a microphone, and do not put that spot out at the 25.

**Separate hard playing from hard moving.** When the winds have difficult notes, park them in a tight pod and let the guard or a non-playing section carry the visual.

**Keep step sizes playable.** We do not write a step bigger than 6 to 5 for a high school band.

**No scatter.** Moving between forms by wandering reads as unfinished. Use a short, timed, staggered step-off into a real form.

**Design the hardest visual section first,** because it will get the most rehearsal, and make the ending something that can be added late.

**Make effects unmissable.** We once opened a show on a set meant to read as a symbol, and nobody in the stands saw it. If an effect matters, plan it in the show document with the music, and do not add choreography after the drill is written.

Small bands have their own staging problem: the field is 100 yards wide and they are not. The fix is to shrink the stage.

![A football field diagram showing a small band staged between the 35-yard lines near the front, with static props on the back hash at the 20s and 40s framing the picture](fig-small-band-staging.svg "Shrinking the stage. The band stays forward and inside the 35s; static props fill the back of the field for a high press box.")

Put working props inside the 35s and use the rest as scenery at the edges. Static props on the back hash fill the field for a judge in a high press box without sending a single student back there. And small bands should not move props: it always looks like logistics. Where a prop starts is where it ends.

Before you design any prop, check four things: the width of the stadium gate, the wind, where it will be stored, and who is building it. We have seen sail props built wider than the gate.

## 8. Design the sound with the music

A clean sound system is now the baseline. A band whose microphones hum has lost credit before the first note is judged, and a band with a polished mix has told the panel it is a serious program.

- **Amplify for presence, not volume.** A pair of shotgun microphones and some reinforcement make a small band sound far bigger than it is.
- **One synth is enough for a 60-member band.** A drone under the opening, a few risers and one impact sample add more than a rack of gear.
- **Write the sound design with the wind book,** not after it. The drone that carries a transition has to be in the score when the transition is written.
- **Run the system on battery** so it is tested in the parking lot before you walk in, and train students to run it.
- **Pay someone once to set levels in a stadium** and teach your staff what they did.

Know your circuit's rules before you write. At Bands of America, all music has to be performed live by a student, sampled wind sounds are not allowed, and tempo-locked loops are not allowed ([2026 BOA handbook, section 22](https://marching.musicforall.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2026/05/2026-BOA.Rulebook-FINAL.pdf)). Texas UIL does not allow prerecorded narration. A sound design that is legal on Saturday in one circuit can draw a penalty in another.

## 9. Know what the sheets pay for

Read the sheets for the circuit you compete in before you design, because they tell you where the points are.

![A bar divided by Bands of America caption weights: Music General Effect 40, Visual General Effect 20, Music Performance 20, Visual Performance 20](fig-boa-weights.svg "Where the 100 points are at a Bands of America event. General effect is 60 of them. Source: 2026 BOA Official Procedures and Adjudication Handbook, section 29.")

At a Bands of America event, general effect is 60 of the 100 points and music general effect alone is 40. Two judges score it and their numbers are added, not averaged. That is the case for spending design time on pacing and on the moments between the movements: it is where the largest caption lives.

What we have seen panels reward, across circuits:

- **Excellence before cool.** The top programs' effect is how well they perform. You cannot out-design the classroom; training sets the ceiling and the show can only reach it.
- **A band that looks and sounds professional gets the benefit of the doubt** on everything else.
- **How the students move.** The better they move, the better every caption scores.
- **Balanced, intentional pictures.** Visual judges mark down uncreative staging and lopsided forms. Once a band's execution rises, panels start asking why each performer is where they are, so have an answer.

Visual judging is partly preference. We have seen one risky small-band show place second with one effect judge and twenty-fifth with another. And unknown bands get marked down. Go to one bigger event each year, stay to watch finals, and the band is better the next season for having seen it.

## 10. The calendar

Two dates hold the year up: all wind music by May 1, and drill by Labor Day.

- **November.** Recap the season by caption with your staff while the tapes are fresh. Decide who is designing next year.
- **December and January.** Concept, playlist, cut tracks, contract. These are an arranger's quiet months, so a show signed in December starts being written within two weeks. Your boosters will want a number before the February budget.
- **By early March.** Music chosen, licensing requests in. A request for an unlisted song goes in the day you choose it, because some publishers take weeks.
- **March and April.** Winds written. Each approved movement goes to the percussion writer as it lands; April and May, after indoor season, is when percussion writers have time.
- **May 1.** All wind music delivered, before spring camp. Read it down with the band, then send edits as one written list.
- **July 1.** Numbers to the drill writer. Drill follows the roster, so it cannot start before you know who you have.
- **Mid-July to Labor Day.** Drill arrives a movement at a time, about every two to three weeks, matched to your camp dates and how many sets a day your band learns. Choreography comes with it, as video.

Choosing a show in March or April is late for custom. If that is where you are, buy a box show that already exists, or take two finished parts and have the rest written around them. Asking in April for music by June 1 cannot be served well by anyone.

One process note. The directors who were unhappy at the end of a season were, almost every time, the ones who gave no feedback on drafts. Fill out the production sheet with your whole staff, and answer when your designer sends you something.

---faq---
Q: How long does it take to design a marching band show?
A: About eight months from the first concept call to the last drill file: concept in December and January, music by May 1, drill by Labor Day.

Q: What is a cut track?
A: The source recordings spliced together at real show length, with transitions and effects roughed in. It lets a staff judge pacing before any music is written, when changes are cheap.

Q: How long should a marching band show be?
A: Plan 6:30 to 7:30. Across the shows in our catalog the median is 7:30 and the range is 5:00 to 8:43. Check your circuit's limits: Bands of America allows 6 to 11 minutes and Texas UIL 5 to 8.

Q: How hard should the music be?
A: Mostly easier than you think, with one or two hard moments chosen on purpose. Plan about 70 percent of rehearsal for those moments and write the rest so it goes on the field quickly.

Q: Should the music or the drill come first?
A: The picture comes first as an idea, the music is written first as a file. Decide what the audience sees in each part before the arranger starts, then write drill to the finished music.

Q: Can one person design the whole show?
A: One person can coordinate it. Winds, percussion, sound design, drill and guard are separate crafts, and what matters is that the people doing them talk to each other from the first call.
