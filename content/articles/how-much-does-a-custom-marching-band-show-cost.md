A custom marching band show is priced as five separate pieces of work: the wind book, percussion, sound design, drill and coordination. Music licensing is a sixth cost that the school usually pays directly. The wind book and the drill are the two largest lines, and a finished box show costs roughly half of what the same show cost to commission.

We quote each program individually, so there is no price list on this page. What is here is how the total is built, which choices move it, and how to get a real number before your boosters vote.

## The five lines on a quote, and the one that is not on it

![A bar showing how a full custom design budget typically divides: wind book about a third, drill a bit over a quarter, percussion about a sixth, licensing about an eighth, sound design about a tenth](fig-cost-shares.svg "How a full custom package typically divides, from the ranges we have quoted. Your shares will differ: a band with its own percussion writer drops a line, and a show of public-domain music drops another.")

**The wind book.** Brass and woodwind arrangements for every part of the show, scored for your instrumentation. This is the core of every quote and usually the largest line. It is priced mostly by band size and difficulty.

**Percussion.** Battery and front ensemble books. This line has the widest range of any, because the work varies the most: a pulse-only book for a first-year bass line is a different job from a battery book for a line that competes indoors. Match the writer to the need. Do not pay a top battery writer for a simple book.

**Sound design.** The synth patches, samples and effects, and the files to run them. Small if you want a drone and a few impacts; larger if the show leans on electronics throughout.

**Drill.** Charts for your exact numbers on your field. Close to the wind book in size. Many directors do not know the going rate for drill because they have only ever paid one person for it, so ask for it as its own line.

**Coordination.** One person who runs the design: the concept, the calendar, the hand-offs between writers and the check-ins during the season. Often bundled with drill.

**Licensing, the line that is not on the quote.** Every copyrighted piece needs a custom arrangement license, charged per song. The fee is set by the publisher and usually invoiced to the school, so it sits outside the design fee. Most directors we talk to already budget it separately. Recent hits cost more than older songs, and music published before 1931 is in the public domain in the United States and needs no license at all ([Duke Center for the Study of the Public Domain](https://cspd.law.duke.edu/publicdomainday/2026/)).

## What moves the number

In rough order of effect:

1. **How many of the five lines you need.** A band with a percussion writer and a drill writer on staff buys a wind book and nothing else. Keeping a trusted local percussion writer who knows your kids is a common and sensible choice.
2. **How much of the music is new.** A show that reuses two finished arrangements and adds two new ones costs less than four new ones. A finished show bought as written costs about half of a commission.
3. **Band size.** More players means more parts, more doublings and more drill dots.
4. **Difficulty.** A grade 5 book takes longer to write and check than a grade 2 book.
5. **How many copyrighted songs are in the show.** A show built on four pop songs carries four license fees. A multi-movement classical work gives you more material for one fee, or for none if it is in the public domain.
6. **How late you start.** Rushed work costs more, or does not get done.

## What is not in a design quote at all

Directors who are new to custom design are sometimes surprised by what the design fee does not include. Budget these separately:

- **Flags, costumes and props.** Materials and construction. One of our clients had a little over a thousand dollars for flags, so they combined a set they owned, a stock set and one custom set.
- **Choreography and guard design,** if someone outside your staff writes it.
- **Sound equipment.** Microphones, a mixer, speakers, and someone to set levels once in a stadium.
- **Transport.** One director told us buses run about as much per local contest as some design lines, because the district has no drivers. A regional trip was dropped on bus cost alone.
- **Staff and clinicians.**
- **Entry fees.**

A show you can afford to design and cannot afford to stage is a common mistake. Before you commit to a concept, price the props and costumes it needs.

## How the money usually works

**The boosters approve it.** In most programs we work with, the booster board votes on the design budget, and the treasurer or president may co-sign the contract. Some districts fund design directly. Some pay from staff stipends that are released late in the fall.

**You need a number before the vote.** Ask in December. A designer can give a range by email if you send your band size, instrumentation, difficulty and which lines you need, and a firm quote after one call.

**Ask for it itemised.** Each line priced separately, so you can take one off if the total is over.

**Ask for a draft contract.** Boards vote more easily on a document than on an email.

**Ask about timing.** Deposits typically run from a quarter to half, with the balance on delivery. If your money arrives in March, say so; the schedule can usually be built around it.

**Ask what happens at renewal.** A price that stays flat for a returning program is worth more than a first-year discount.

## Three budgets, as shapes

These are the three shapes we see most. They are described by what is in them, not by a total.

**A young band that plays Friday nights first.** Wind book and simple sound design, with licensing included in the planning. Percussion is written in-house. Drill is written by the director. This is the smallest custom budget, and it works because the show is short and the music is written to go on the field fast.

**A competitive band of 60 to 80.** Wind book, percussion, sound design, drill and coordination from one team. This is the full package, and it is where the shares in the figure above come from.

**A program moving up from box shows.** A finished show re-scored for the band, one new part written, drill for the band's numbers. Less than a commission, with most of the fit.

## Where a custom budget is wasted

**On a show the band cannot teach.** A designer we learned from puts it this way: a band that cannot yet teach a custom show is better served by a finished one in hand early, with the difference spent on instruments and clinicians.

**On writing that is too hard.** You pay for the writing and then pay again in rehearsal time.

**On late delivery.** Music that arrives in July has cost you the spring, whatever the invoice says.

**On parts you rewrite.** If your staff rewrites drill pages because the step sizes hurt the playing, you bought drill twice.

When directors describe a bad year with a designer, they rarely mention the price. They talk about music that was late and designers who never spoke to each other.

## Ways to bring the total down without hurting the show

- Reuse a finished opener or ballad and commission the rest.
- Choose public-domain music for one or two parts.
- Keep percussion in-house if you have the writer.
- Shorten the show. Fewer minutes is less music, less drill and less to rehearse.
- Design props that stay still. They are cheaper to build and need no crew.
- Sign early, when you have the most choice.

---faq---
Q: Can I get a ballpark before a call?
A: Yes. Send your band size, instrumentation, difficulty level and which pieces of work you need, and a designer can reply with a range.

Q: Is drill included in a custom show?
A: Only if you ask for it. Drill is its own line on a quote.

Q: Who pays for music licensing?
A: Usually the school, directly to the licensing agent or publisher. It is separate from the design fee.

Q: Is a box show cheaper than a custom show?
A: Yes, roughly half, because the writing is already done. Re-scoring one for your band or adding a new part sits in between.

Q: Why is percussion priced so differently from band to band?
A: Because the work varies more than any other line, from a simple pulse book to a full competitive battery and front ensemble book.

Q: When should I ask for a quote?
A: In December, so the number is ready before your boosters set the budget.
