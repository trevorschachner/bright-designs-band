A small band needs a show that was written for a small band: a shorter show, scored in three lines instead of ten, staged on a smaller piece of the field, with the best few players at microphones. A big-band show with parts crossed out gives you the big band's problems and none of its sound.

This is what we have learned writing for bands of 20 to 60, and what we tell directors who ask how a band their size can look bigger.

## What small means depends on who is judging

Before you pick a show, check how your circuit sorts bands, because small is not one thing.

| Circuit | How bands are classed | What that means for a 45-member band |
|---|---|---|
| Bands of America | School enrollment, grades 10 to 12. Class A is 600 students or fewer. | Class is about your school, not your band. Finals are the top scores regardless of class. |
| Tournament of Bands | Number of musicians. Group 1 is up to 30, Group 2 is 31 to 50. | You compete with bands your size. |
| Florida (FMBC) | Total members, guard included. 1A is 1 to 50. | You compete with bands your size. |
| South Carolina (SCBDA) | School enrollment rank, six classes. | A small band at a big school is in a big class. |
| Texas (UIL) | Conference by school size. | Same: school size, not band size. |

Sources: [2026 BOA handbook](https://marching.musicforall.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2026/05/2026-BOA.Rulebook-FINAL.pdf), section 1.03, and each circuit's published classification rules.

So the same 45 students are a mid-size band on Saturday in one circuit and the smallest band in the stadium the next week in another. Design for the hardest room you will play in. That is usually the one with the highest press box.

## Why a thinned-out big-band show fails

A show written for 150 assumes things a small band does not have.

It assumes a unison hit lands. With 150 players it does. With 40, one note in octaves sounds thin, and the same moment needs a full chord to feel like an impact.

It assumes sections can carry long lines. Eight trumpets can stagger-breathe through a sixteen-bar sustain. Three cannot, and you hear every breath.

It assumes the drill fills the field. Forms drawn for 150 dots leave holes when a third of them are missing, and a small band spread from 20 to 20 looks like a rehearsal with half the kids absent.

And it assumes depth on the bench. When a show has four hard moments and one of your two good trombones is out with the flu, there is nobody behind him.

## A shorter show, on purpose

The 11 shows in our catalog that were written for small bands run a median of 6:45. The rest of the catalog runs 7:30. The difference is almost all in the closer: 1:30 for the small-band shows against about two minutes for the others.

![Two bars comparing median show length: shows written for small bands 6:45, the rest of the catalog 7:30, with the closer marked as the part that shrinks](fig-small-band-length.svg "Median length of the 11 small-band shows in our catalog against the other 40. The closer does most of the shrinking.")

That is deliberate. Every minute of show is rehearsal you have to find, and a small band has fewer people to carry each minute. A director with around 100 members and no drumline told us he cut a whole section mid-season to get inside his time limit. Another never finished teaching the last movement's choreography; five fewer sets would have fixed it.

Check your circuit's minimum (Bands of America is 6 minutes, UIL is 5), then plan to sit comfortably above it and no higher. Thirty extra seconds earns nothing.

## Score in three lines

For a band under about 24 winds, stop thinking soprano, alto, tenor, bass and think high, middle, low.

![A score reduced to three lines, High, Middle and Low, showing which instruments share each line and where the front ensemble and synth fill in](fig-three-lines.svg "Three lines instead of ten. Every instrument is on a part that somebody else is also playing.")

Each instrument joins one of three lines, and there are rarely more than three parts sounding at once. A 16-wind band might be six high, five middle and five low. Nobody is alone on a part, so one absence does not take a note out of the chord. Mark Lortz makes the same case in [The Instrumentalist](https://theinstrumentalist.com/may-2019/arranging-music-for-small-marching-bands/): for bands under 30, one part per section and three lines of melody, countermelody and bass.

The rest of how we write a small-band book:

- **Thicker at the hits.** A hit needs more notes in the chord, not more volume from the same few players.
- **Sustains go to the front ensemble and the synth.** Long chords live in vibes, marimba rolls and a pad, so the winds can play the line and breathe.
- **Every section gets the tune, in short phrases.** Four bars in the trumpets, four in the saxes, four in the low brass. No section carries a melody for a whole part, and the band sounds like a band.
- **Saxes cover what is missing.** Altos double a thin mellophone line. Tenors and baris reinforce low brass.
- **Friendly keys.** F, B-flat and E-flat, and about three tempos in the whole show.
- **Save the brass.** With six brass players, loud is an effect you get to use a few times. Keep them out of the texture so the impact means something.
- **Feature euphonium before tuba.** A euphonium carries an extended solo across a stadium. A tuba feature works as a short moment with a wireless microphone in the bell.

Most of the music in our small-band shows is grade 1 to 2: 24 of the 38 parts, with the rest at grade 3 to 4. Directors ask for exactly that mix. Grade 2 ensemble parts, with hard solos for the two or three players who can play them.

## You do not have to ask permission

The move that changes the most for a small band is the one directors feel least allowed to make: take your best one to five players, stand them still at microphones, and let them carry the show.

A national finalist stood a one-on-a-part woodwind group at microphones for ninety seconds while the rest of the band moved behind them. It is not a concession to size. It keeps your best sound near the front, it gives your weaker players less to carry, and the soloists practise alone so it costs you no ensemble rehearsal.

Three things make it work.

**Give each soloist one fixed spot.** One microphone, one place, every time. Do not put a soloist out at the 25 where the audience has to find them.

**Vary the pattern.** Judges mark small bands down for repeating soloist, then band, then soloist, then band, even though bigger bands are praised for the same thing. Use a duet. Use a small ensemble. Let the soloist play over the full band instead of before it.

**Know the amplification rule.** Bands of America restricts amplifying individuals during full-band passages, and makes a stated exception for small bands with about one player per part, as long as the speakers carry the true sound of the ensemble ([2026 handbook, section 22 policy statement](https://marching.musicforall.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/5/2026/05/2026-BOA.Rulebook-FINAL.pdf)). Read your own circuit's rule before you buy microphones.

## Shrink the stage

Directors of small bands hear the same two comments from the box. First: the show is good but too far away. Then, after they move everything forward: it looks crushed. One band was praised for its staging all season and marked down at state, because the state press box was much higher and the judges could see all the empty field behind the band.

The answer to both is to make the stage smaller instead of making the band cover more of it.

![A football field diagram showing a small band staged between the 35-yard lines near the front, with static props on the back hash at the 20s and 40s framing the picture](../how-to-design-a-marching-band-show/fig-small-band-staging.svg "The band stays forward and inside the 35s. Static props fill the back of the field for a high press box.")

- **Stay inside the 35s and in front of the back hash.** That is your stage. The rest of the field is scenery.
- **Put static props deep.** Flats or frames on the back hash at the 20s and 40s fill the picture from a high box, and no student has to go back there.
- **Frame the edges.** Unused prop pieces at the sides tell the eye where the stage ends.
- **Do not move props.** With a small band it always looks like logistics. Where a prop starts is where it ends.
- **Do not go deep,** with one exception: the start of the ballad, when a soloist is playing and the band has time to regroup.
- **Keep the battery central,** especially a young one.
- **Under-write the drill.** If you are not sure of your numbers, write drill for the students you are sure of. Extras come on only for the impacts and wait behind a prop between entrances. A guard that shrinks from twelve to eight mid-season will not leave one performer alone on one side of the field.

Cheap things that read as a bigger production: painted rope, holographic tape, a highlight colour that already matches the uniform, one or two new flags instead of three sets, nesting four-foot cubes, a fabric flyover that folds into a bin. One of our clients could afford one custom set of flags, not three. They used one set they owned, one stock set and one custom set.

## Sound: presence, not volume

A small band does not need to be loud. It needs to sound close.

- **Two shotgun microphones and some reinforcement** make 40 players sound far larger without sounding amplified.
- **One synth is enough.** A drone under the opening, a few risers and one impact sample.
- **Synth bass or electric bass** fills the bottom when you have one tuba.
- **Train a student to run it,** and have someone other than the percussion director at the board.
- **Have a daylight plan.** Lighting effects that looked good in the gym disappear in an afternoon performance slot.

If your battery is under six players, consider going without one. Below that number a field battery struggles to sound cohesive. An all-front percussion section with a drum set, a few bass drums on stands, mallets and a synth can carry the pulse better, and some small programs compete that way.

## Picking the concept

Three things we have seen hold up with small bands.

**Fun shows with music the crowd knows.** They build buy in, the Friday night crowd responds, and eighth graders decide to join. Mature material can come later, when the teaching is in place.

**No single lead character.** If the show depends on one performer and that student moves in September, you have no show.

**Be careful with dark.** Several staffs told us dark, serious shows are harder to sell with a small band. The weight has to come from somewhere, and 40 players cannot always supply it. A lighter world, performed well, scores better than a heavy one performed thinly.

## Questions to ask before you buy any show for a small band

1. What is the smallest instrumentation it has been performed with?
2. How many separate wind parts are there, and can they be reduced to three lines?
3. Where do the sustained chords live: in the winds, or in the front ensemble and synth?
4. Is there a version with no battery?
5. Who are the soloists, and is there an easier version of each solo?
6. How long is it, and can the closer be cut without a rewrite?
7. Will the seller re-score it for the instruments you have?

If the answer to the last one is no, keep looking. A box show that is re-scored to your numbers, with one piece swapped for something your kids love, is most of the way to a custom show at a fraction of the wait.

---faq---
Q: How many members is a small marching band?
A: It depends on the circuit. Tournament of Bands calls 30 musicians or fewer Group 1; Florida's FMBC calls 50 total members or fewer 1A. Bands of America and most state associations class by school enrollment, so a small band can be in a large class. For writing music, under five winds per part is a useful line.

Q: Can a small band compete without a drumline?
A: Yes. With fewer than about six battery players, a front-ensemble-only percussion section often sounds better and is easier to keep in time.

Q: How long should a small band's show be?
A: Around 6:30 to 7:00. The small-band shows in our catalog run a median of 6:45, with a closer of about 1:30.

Q: How do we make a small band look bigger?
A: Shrink the stage. Stay inside the 35-yard lines and near the front, put static props on the back hash to fill the field, and do not move them.

Q: Is it legal to mic individual players?
A: It depends on the circuit. Bands of America limits individual amplification during full-band passages and makes an exception for bands with about one player per part. Check your own rulebook.

Q: Should a small band play easier music?
A: Mostly, yes: grade 2 ensemble writing that goes on the field quickly, plus hard solos for the players who can handle them.
